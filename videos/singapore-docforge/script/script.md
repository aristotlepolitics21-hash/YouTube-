# How Singapore Got Rich With Nothing

## The hook

In 1965, a small island was pushed out of a country it had joined less than two years earlier. It had no oil, no farmland, no hinterland, and not even enough fresh water for its own people. Its own prime minister broke down on television as he announced the news. Today, its people are among the richest in the world. Its port is one of the busiest anywhere, and its airport connects the planet. How did a tiny country with almost nothing become this? This is the story of Singapore.

## Why Singapore matters

Singapore's entire land area is less than seven hundred and fifty square kilometres. About six million people live on it. In 1965, Singapore's output per person was about five hundred dollars a year. By 2024, it was more than ninety thousand dollars. That is about a hundred and eighty times as much, before adjusting for inflation. Few countries have ever made that journey from poverty to wealth so quickly. And Singapore did it without the things that usually make countries rich: oil, minerals, land. So what did it have instead?

## Origins

Start with location. Singapore sits at the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula, where the Strait of Malacca opens toward the South China Sea. For centuries, ships sailing between India, China and the Middle East have passed through these waters. In January 1819, an official of the British East India Company, Stamford Raffles, arrived on the island. Britain wanted a port on this route that its Dutch rivals, the main European power in the region, could not control. At the time, perhaps a thousand people lived there. On the sixth of February, he signed a treaty with the local rulers that allowed the British to set up a trading post. Singapore became a free port, open to traders from everywhere. Treaties signed in 1824, with the Dutch and with the local rulers, made the island British. Merchants and workers came from China, India, the Malay world and far beyond, and the town grew quickly. Rubber and tin from the Malay Peninsula flowed out through its harbour, and goods from Europe and India flowed in. In 1826, it became part of the Straits Settlements, and ten years later their capital. For more than a century, Singapore was a busy colonial port, built on one thing: trade.

## War, merger and separation

That world collapsed in February 1942. Japanese forces crossed the narrow Johor Strait and attacked the island from the north-west. On the fifteenth of February, the British commander surrendered. About eighty thousand British, Indian, Australian and local troops became prisoners of war. Singapore was renamed Syonan-to, and endured three and a half years of occupation. In the first weeks, thousands of Chinese civilians were rounded up and killed in a purge known as Sook Ching. Food ran short, and many families survived on tapioca and sweet potatoes. When the British returned in September 1945, the old idea that they could always protect the colony was gone. The years that followed brought strikes, riots and a long struggle over who would govern the colony. In 1959, Singapore won self-government, and Lee Kuan Yew of the People's Action Party became its first prime minister. His government believed the island was too small to survive on its own. So in September 1963, Singapore joined the new Federation of Malaysia. It did not last. There were deep political disagreements, and deadly racial riots in 1964. On the ninth of August 1965, Singapore separated from Malaysia and became an independent country. Lee Kuan Yew called it a moment of anguish. He had believed in merger all his adult life. Singapore was now a nation. Whether it could survive was a different question.

## An island with nothing

Look at what the new country had to work with. The main island is only about fifty kilometres from east to west. Much of it was swamp, forest or crowded town, with very little room to spare. No oil. No gas. No minerals worth mining. No room for large farms. Worst of all, not enough water for a growing city. Since 1961 and 1962, Singapore had depended on agreements to buy water from the Malaysian state of Johor, across the strait. Its neighbours, Malaysia and Indonesia, were far larger, and relations in the region were tense. Its population, about two million people, was crowded, mostly poor, and growing. Many lived in crowded shophouses and wooden villages, and jobs were scarce.

## The turning point

Then, in January 1968, came a second shock. Britain announced it would pull its military forces out of the region by 1971. Its bases in Singapore generated around a fifth of the economy. They employed tens of thousands of local workers and occupied about twelve percent of the island's land. Singapore had already lost its hoped-for common market when it left Malaysia. Now it was about to lose its protector and one of its biggest employers. The government moved fast. In 1967 it began national service, to build an army of its own. In 1968, new labour laws limited strikes and gave employers more control, to make the island more attractive to investors. And in 1971, a long-term Concept Plan mapped out land, roads, housing and industry for decades ahead. The government had to decide what kind of economy this island would have.

## The economic transformation

Part of the answer was already under way. In 1961, a United Nations team led by the Dutch economist Albert Winsemius had advised Singapore to industrialise, fast. That year, the government set up the Economic Development Board to win investment and create jobs. Its first big project was a vast industrial estate on swampy land at Jurong, in the west of the island. Many newly independent countries at the time were wary of foreign multinational companies. Singapore did the opposite. It invited them in. Foreign companies brought money, technology and know-how, and Singaporean workers learned on the job. Factories making clothing, electronics and parts for global brands opened across the island, and jobs followed. Oil companies built refineries here too. Shell opened the first, on the island of Pulau Bukom, in 1961. A country with no oil of its own became one of Asia's great refining centres. Trade kept growing too, and Singapore's harbour became one of the busiest ports on the planet. In 2024, it handled a record forty one million containers, counted in twenty foot units. About nine in ten are transshipped, moved from one ship to another on the way somewhere else. In 1981, Changi Airport opened, making Singapore a hub of the air as well as the sea. Singapore Airlines, formed in 1972, carried the country's name around the world. Over the decades, the economy climbed up the value chain, into chemicals, precision engineering, finance and semiconductors. Manufacturing is still about a fifth of the economy, and around one in every ten chips made in the world is made in Singapore.

## The key policies

Investors did not come only for the location. They came because the government worked, and because it was clean. Civil servants were judged on results, and the rules on bribery applied all the way up. The streets were cleaned up, and littering was punished with fines. An anti-corruption agency, set up in 1952, was given tough new laws in 1960 to go after bribery at every level. In 2024, Transparency International ranked Singapore the third least corrupt country in the world. Then there was housing. In 1960, the government created the Housing and Development Board to replace overcrowded slums with modern flats. In May 1961, a huge fire in Bukit Ho Swee left about sixteen thousand people homeless. The board rehoused them and kept building at remarkable speed: twenty one thousand flats in less than three years. From 1968, workers could use savings from the Central Provident Fund, a compulsory savings scheme, to buy their homes. Today, about seventy seven percent of residents live in public housing, and around nine in ten own their home. Owning a home gave ordinary people a direct stake in the country's success. The state saved and invested too. Temasek Holdings was set up in 1974, and GIC in 1981, to manage its assets and reserves. And it chose English as a common working language, alongside each community's mother tongue. None of this was gentle. The government was strict, and critics point to tight limits on the press and the political opposition.

## People and society

Singapore's real resource has always been its people. Most are of Chinese, Malay or Indian descent, alongside many other communities. Within a single generation, many families moved from villages and crowded shophouses into high-rise flats. Each has left its mark on the city's neighbourhoods, festivals and food. Public housing estates use ethnic quotas, so that communities live side by side rather than apart. Hawker centres, where cheap meals from every tradition are sold under one roof, became part of the national identity. In 2020, UNESCO recognised Singapore's hawker culture as part of humanity's intangible heritage. Education became a national priority, and Singapore's students now regularly score near the top of international tests. Today, about six million people live on the island, and nearly a third of them are not citizens or permanent residents.

## A global role

For a country this small, Singapore's reach is enormous. Its total trade is worth more than three times its whole economy. It is one of the world's leading financial centres, ranked in the global top five. Hundreds of global companies run their Asian operations from the island. And Changi has been voted the world's best airport many times. Its shipping lanes, airport and banks tie Southeast Asia to the rest of the world. And it works hard to stay on good terms with both the United States and China.

## The problems

Success has brought problems of its own. Singapore is regularly ranked among the most expensive cities in the world to live in. Its people are having very few children. In 2023, the fertility rate fell below one child per woman for the first time. About one in five citizens is now sixty five or older, and by 2030 it will be one in four. Land is still scarce, and the economy relies heavily on foreign workers. Housing prices have climbed, and many young couples worry about whether they can afford a home. And as a low-lying island, Singapore is exposed to rising seas, which could rise by up to a metre by the end of the century. In 2019, the prime minister said protecting the coast could cost a hundred billion Singapore dollars or more over the coming decades. And some Singaporeans ask whether a system built for growth leaves enough room for dissent.

## The future

So Singapore is doing what it has always done: planning decades ahead. It has grown its own land by more than a quarter since the 1960s, by reclaiming it from the sea. Off the island of Pulau Tekong, it has built its first polder, Dutch style: land below sea level, protected by a wall. A giant new port is rising at Tuas, designed to handle sixty five million containers a year when it is finished in the 2040s. At Changi, a fifth terminal is under construction, planned to open in the mid 2030s. For water, it has built its own supply: rainwater, recycled water called NEWater, and desalination plants. The first water agreement with Malaysia ended in 2011. The last one runs until 2061.

## Conclusion

Singapore began with no oil, no farmland and not enough water. What it had was a location, and leaders who treated that location as a starting point, not a guarantee. It invited the world in, housed its people, fought corruption, and saved for the future. Its success came from choices: to stay open, to plan ahead, and to keep its promises to investors and to its own people. None of it was inevitable. In 1965, even its founders were not sure it would survive. Resources matter. But Singapore shows that what a country does with its position can matter even more.
