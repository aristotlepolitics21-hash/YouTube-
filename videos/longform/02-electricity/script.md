# The Scientist Who Discovered Electricity

Format: 3D CGI explainer, 16:9, about 10 minutes (target 1,450 spoken words at ~145 wpm).
Visual plan per paragraph is in [brackets]; these become the shot specs.

---

## 1. Hook (0:00)

Every light you have ever switched on, every phone you have ever charged, every train, fridge and hospital machine depends on one trick. [Glowing city at night, camera pulls back from a light bulb.]

It's a trick so simple you could do it with a magnet and a coil of wire. [Hands push a magnet into a copper coil; a needle flicks.]

And it was found by a poor bookbinder's boy who left school at thirteen, never learned advanced maths, and was once told he was little more than a servant. [3D boy among stacks of books; a candle.]

His name was Michael Faraday. This is how he turned electricity from a party trick into the power that runs the world. [Title card: THE SCIENTIST WHO DISCOVERED ELECTRICITY.]

## 2. A spark nobody understood (0:40)

People had seen electricity for thousands of years. Around 600 BC, Greek thinkers noticed that rubbed amber could pick up feathers and dust. [Amber rod, feathers lifting.] The Greek word for amber was "elektron". [3D text ELEKTRON.]

In 1600, the English doctor William Gilbert used that word to coin a new Latin term, "electricus". [Old book opening.]

In 1752, Benjamin Franklin flew a kite into a storm and showed that lightning was electricity too. [Storm, kite, key, spark.]

Then in 1800, Alessandro Volta stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by brine-soaked cloth, and made the first battery, a steady flow of current. [Voltaic pile building disc by disc, glowing current.]

But it was weak, expensive, and nobody really knew what it was. Electricity was a curiosity for lecture halls, not a power source. [Audience in a lecture hall gasping at sparks.]

## 3. The bookbinder's apprentice (1:50)

Michael Faraday was born in 1791 near London, the son of a blacksmith. His family was poor; at times he had a single loaf of bread to last a week. [Small figure in a grey street, bread loaf.]

At fourteen he became an apprentice to a bookbinder. And instead of just binding the books, he read them. [Bookbinding bench, books flying open.]

One of them was an encyclopedia with a long article on electricity. Faraday built his own simple electrical machines from scraps, in the back of the shop. [Homemade jar and wires on a workbench, small sparks.]

In 1812, a customer gave him tickets to hear the most famous chemist in Britain, Sir Humphry Davy, at the Royal Institution. [Grand lecture theatre, Davy at the bench.]

Faraday took careful notes, rewrote them neatly, bound them into a book, and sent it to Davy, asking for a job. [Hand-bound notebook, ribbon, sent across the desk.]

A few months later, a lab assistant was sacked after a fight, and Davy hired Faraday to wash bottles. [Bottles, glassware, a mop.]

## 4. The assistant who outgrew his master (3:00)

Faraday worked his way up. He travelled across Europe with Davy, sometimes treated like a valet, but meeting the greatest scientists of the age. [Map of Europe with a moving coach.]

Then, in 1820, the Danish scientist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed something strange: a wire carrying current made a nearby compass needle swing. [Compass needle deflecting beside a glowing wire.]

Electricity could create magnetism. The scientific world went wild. [Ripples of light, newspapers.]

In 1821, Faraday built a small device: a wire dipped in a cup of mercury, hanging beside a magnet. When current flowed, the wire spun round and round the magnet. [Wire tip circling the magnet in a glowing cup.]

Electricity had been turned into continuous motion. It was the ancestor of every electric motor. [Spinning motor in a modern fan or car.]

But instead of praise, Faraday faced suspicion. Some, Davy among them, felt he had used another scientist's ideas without credit. It hurt him deeply. [Dark room, Faraday alone, a single lamp.]

## 5. Ten years of failure (4:20)

Faraday became convinced of something. If electricity could make magnetism, then magnetism should be able to make electricity. [Two arrows: electricity to magnet, magnet to question mark.]

For years he tried. He put magnets next to wires. Nothing. He wound wires around magnets. Nothing. He kept going between other work, including liquefying chlorine and discovering benzene. [Montage of failed experiments; needles not moving.]

## 6. August 29, 1831 (5:00)

Then, on the 29th of August, 1831, he took an iron ring and wrapped two separate coils of wire around it. One coil was connected to a battery. The other was connected to a needle that detected current. [Iron ring with two copper coils, battery, galvanometer.]

When he connected the battery, the needle jumped... and then fell back to zero. When he disconnected it, the needle jumped the other way. [Needle flick, return, flick.]

The current only appeared when the magnetism was changing. [Field lines growing and collapsing through the ring.]

Weeks later, he pushed a bar magnet into a coil of wire. The needle moved. He pulled it out. The needle moved the other way. Moving magnets made electricity. [Magnet into coil, needle moves, glowing current.]

This is electromagnetic induction. And it's the trick behind nearly every power station on Earth. [Turbine hall.]

## 7. The first generator (6:00)

Faraday then spun a copper disc between the poles of a magnet. A steady current flowed out of it, for as long as the disc kept turning. [Copper disc spinning between a horseshoe magnet, sliding contacts.]

It was the first electric generator. Turn something, and you get electricity. It didn't matter what did the turning: water, steam, wind, or later, nuclear heat. [Disc becomes turbine; water, steam, wind icons.]

Today almost all the electricity we use comes from Faraday's idea: a magnet and a coil of wire moving past each other, millions of times a second, in giant turbines. [Inside a modern generator, magnets spinning past coils.]

## 8. Invisible lines of force (6:50)

Faraday could not do advanced maths. So he imagined what he could not calculate. [Faraday with iron filings on paper.]

He sprinkled iron filings around magnets and saw them line up into curved patterns. He believed these lines of force were real things filling the space around magnets and currents. [Iron filings snapping into field lines.]

Most scientists thought this was childish. But a young Scottish physicist, James Clerk Maxwell, turned Faraday's pictures into equations in the 1860s. [Glowing equations appear over the field lines.]

Those equations showed that light itself is a wave of electricity and magnetism. Radio, Wi-Fi and mobile phones all come from that insight. [Light wave, then radio waves from a tower to a phone.]

## 9. The man who said no (7:50)

Faraday became famous. He also discovered that a metal cage shields what's inside from electric fields, which is why we now call it a Faraday cage. [Metal cage with sparks hitting outside, figure safe inside.]

He began the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for children, which still run today. [Children in the lecture theatre, candle demonstration.]

But he turned down a knighthood, and twice refused to become President of the Royal Society. He said he wanted to remain plain Michael Faraday to the last. [Crown and medal declined, simple coat.]

## 10. Legacy (8:40)

Faraday died in 1867. A few years later, Edison and others used generators built on his principle to light whole streets. [Victorian street lamps lighting one by one.]

The unit of electrical capacitance, the farad, is named after him. [3D text: 1 FARAD.]

Albert Einstein kept a picture of Faraday on his study wall. [Study with three portraits.]

## 11. Close (9:20)

He didn't discover electricity. Nobody did. But Michael Faraday discovered how to make it, how to move it, and how to think about it. [Spark to coil to turbine to city.]

So the next time you flip a switch, remember the bookbinder's boy with a magnet and a coil of wire. [Hand flips a switch, the room lights up; logo.]
