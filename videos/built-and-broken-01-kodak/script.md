# Built & Broken, Episode 1: Kodak (fact-checked)

**Title:** Kodak Saw the Future in 1975 and Hid It
**Thumbnail:** DON'T TELL ANYONE.


## The hook

In 1975, an engineer at Kodak built the world's first digital camera.

Kodak had the invention. It had the patents. It had the money, the brand, and the distribution in nearly every country on Earth.

And thirty-seven years later, it filed for bankruptcy.

This is not the story of a company that missed the future. Kodak saw it first.

This is the story of a company that saw the future clearly, and chose the past anyway.


## The rise

In the 1880s, photography was for experts. You needed glass plates, chemicals, and patience.

George Eastman changed that. In 1888 he launched a simple box camera with a promise: you press the button, we do the rest.

You took the pictures, mailed the whole camera back, and got your prints and a reloaded camera in return.

In 1900 came the Brownie, priced at one dollar. Photography was now for everyone.

But the real genius was the business model. Kodak sold cameras cheaply, almost as bait.

The profit was in the film, the chemicals, and the paper. Every photo someone took paid Kodak again.

By 1976, Kodak sold about 90 percent of the film and 85 percent of the cameras in the United States.

Kodak moment entered the language. The yellow box was one of the most recognisable things in millions of households.

Remember this model. It made Kodak rich. It is also what would trap it.


## The invention

In 1975, a young Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson was handed a loose assignment: see what you can do with a new kind of sensor called a CCD.

He built a camera from a lens, a handful of circuit boards, and a cassette tape.

It weighed about eight pounds. It captured a black and white image of 0.01 megapixels, and it took 23 seconds to record one photo.

Sasson showed it to Kodak's executives. As he later told the New York Times, it was filmless photography, so the reaction was: that's cute, but don't tell anyone about it.

It wasn't stupidity. It was arithmetic. Kodak earned its profits from film.

A camera that needed no film was not a gift. It was a threat to the company's own profit engine.


## The warning signs

Here is the part people get wrong. Kodak did not ignore digital.

It poured money into it. It built a portfolio of more than a thousand digital imaging patents.

In 2005, Kodak was the number one seller of digital cameras in the United States. It had even bought an online photo sharing site, Ofoto, in 2001.

But look at how it used these things. Kodak treated digital as a way to sell more prints. Ofoto existed to get you to order paper photos.

The company was still running the old razor and blades model on new technology.

The problem: digital cameras had no film margin. Every digital camera Kodak sold meant fewer rolls of film sold.

So inside the company, digital was always competing with the product that paid everyone's salaries.

Meanwhile, rival Fuji went the other way. It cut back its own film business, moved into chemicals, cosmetics, and medical imaging, and survived.

And then the real disruptor arrived. The phone.


## The fall

Once cameras were built into phones, the standalone camera market collapsed. Film sales fell off a cliff.

Kodak's profits, built on decades of film, shrank year after year.

In January 2012, Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

Three months later, Facebook bought Instagram, a photo sharing app with 13 employees, for about one billion dollars.

A company that had defined photography for a century was in bankruptcy court in the same season that the new version of photography got its first big price tag.

Kodak sold its digital imaging patents for about 525 million dollars, and emerged from bankruptcy in 2013 as a much smaller company, focused largely on commercial printing.


## What it cost

At its peak in 1988, Kodak employed about 145,000 people. Entire neighbourhoods in Rochester, New York were built around the company.

Over the decades of its decline, most of those jobs disappeared.


## The lesson

Kodak's fall isn't about a lack of vision. It's about a very hard question: what do you do when the thing that makes you rich is the thing that has to die?

Kodak had the answer in a lab in 1975. It had thirty-seven years to act on it.

Its leaders chose to protect the business they had instead of building the one that was coming.

The warning sign isn't a rival getting stronger. It's the moment you catch yourself hiding your own best idea, because it threatens your profits.
