In November 1945, the United States, Britain, France and the Soviet Union opened the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg. It was the first time the leaders of a defeated regime were tried before an international court.

Twenty-one defendants sat in the dock, and one more was tried in his absence. Every word was translated live into four languages through headphones, and prosecutors relied on the regime's own records. The court rejected "following orders" as a defence. In October 1946, twelve were sentenced to death, seven to prison, and three were acquitted. The principles set out at Nuremberg shaped the Genocide Convention of 1948 and, in 2002, the International Criminal Court.
