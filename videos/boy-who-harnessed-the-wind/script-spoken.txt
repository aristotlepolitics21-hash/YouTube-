You are 14, your family is starving, and you just lost your place at school. Nobody in your village has power. What do you build?
This is the true story of William Kamkwamba, the boy who harnessed the wind. And the part everyone remembers, the windmill, is not the most impressive thing he did.
By the end of this video you will know exactly how he built it, what he built it from, and why the hardest part had nothing to do with engineering.
Malawi, 2001. William lives in a farming village where almost nobody has electricity. When the sun goes down, the day is over. His family grows maize. That is their food and their income.
Then the rains fail. A drought, and then a famine. Families cut down to one meal a day. William's father can no longer pay his school fees, so William has to leave school.
For most kids, that is the end of the story. Here is where William's begins.
He can't go to class, so he goes to the small library at his old primary school. He doesn't have the English to read most of the books, so he reads the pictures. Diagrams. And one book has a picture of a windmill on the cover.
The book is called Using Energy. And it explains something nobody around him had told him: wind can turn a blade, the blade can turn a generator, and a generator makes electricity.
So William does the maths a hungry kid does. Electricity means light at night. It means a radio. And if it can pump water, it means a second harvest in the dry season. Which means the famine doesn't have to happen again.
But he has no money. So he goes to the scrapyard.
Here is the parts list, and every piece of it was something other people had thrown away. A broken bicycle frame. A fan blade from an old tractor. PVC pipe, which he heats and flattens into blades. A small bicycle dynamo, the kind that powers a bike light. And for the tower, wood from blue gum trees.
And this is the part I find hardest to imagine. Every day he walks to the dump. The village watches. And they decide he has gone mad. The word they use is misala. Crazy.
He keeps going anyway. No teacher. No internet. A book he can only half read, and parts he can't afford to replace if he breaks them.
Then he raises the tower, and the wind turns the blades, and a single bulb lights up. Then a radio. In a house that has never had either.
The crowd that called him crazy turns up to charge their phones.
Here is the thing the film can't really show you. The windmill was the proof. What William actually did was decide that being out of school was not the same as being finished learning.
Word gets out. A journalist writes about him. In 2007 he is invited to speak at TED, in Tanzania. He has never been on a plane. He has never used a computer. He tells the room, I tried, and I made it.
After that, there is a book, then a place at Dartmouth College in the United States, and in 2019 a Netflix film directed by Chiwetel Ejiofor.
And he went back. He built more windmills, a solar-powered pump for the village well, and he has spent years helping other young people build things of their own.
So that is the story. A bicycle, a tractor fan, a library book, and a kid who kept walking to the dump when everyone was laughing at him.
